Historical Instruments

Early strings

Violoncello da spalla · Viola d'amore · Viola da gamba

This is the work that sets VIOLZ apart. Studying three-hundred-year-old drawings and the style of surviving instruments, I build early strings that breathe in the playing conditions of today. All three are instruments for which specialist makers are hard to find in Korea.

I.

Violoncello da spalla

Violoncello da spalla — “the cello of the shoulder”

A small five-string cello, hung from the shoulder on a strap and held across the body like a violin. Used in Bach's time and then forgotten, it has returned to Baroque stages in recent years, and stands at the centre of the discussion about which instrument Bach's unaccompanied cello suites were written for — particularly the sixth, BWV 1012, which calls for five strings.

It lets violinists and violists sing the cello register directly. Drawing cello depth out of so small a body asks for more precision in plate graduation, arching and string length than a standard instrument does.

II.

Viola d'amore

Viola d'amore — “the viola of love”

Beneath the playing strings, where the bow never reaches, run sympathetic strings that sound of their own accord. Six or seven playing strings and as many sympathetic ones leave behind a halo of overtones that no other string instrument gives. The flame soundholes and the blindfolded head of Amor are its emblems.

Loved by Vivaldi and Telemann, revived by Hindemith — and a demanding instrument to build, since it must carry twice the number of strings while keeping a thin, responsive top. VIOLZ delivers it complete, with the sympathetic strings tuned and the set-up done.

III.

Viola da gamba

Viola da gamba — “the viola of the leg”

Held upright between the legs, this is the heart of the viol family. Sloping shoulders, C-shaped soundholes, a fretted fingerboard like a lute's, six or seven strings — a different lineage from the violin family altogether, and the voice that carried the bass line with such poise in Renaissance and Baroque chamber music.

From the French viol music of Marin Marais and Sainte-Colombe to Bach's gamba sonatas, its nature lies in dynamics fine enough to whisper. The flat back, the light structure — a grammar of construction quite unlike the violin family's — are followed as the historical sources have them.
