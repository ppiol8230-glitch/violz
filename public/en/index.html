Why These Instruments

Why I make these instruments

Music today does not happen only in large halls. There are far more small stages now, close enough to touch the audience, and at that distance the grain of a sound carries further than its volume.

Sympathetic strings that sound without being touched, the overtones gut strings release, a low register travelling up through the player's body — early instruments resonate by a different grammar than modern ones. The surprise of hearing that for the first time is what has kept me at this work.

Most of the music these instruments were born into rang out in churches — Bach's cantatas and Passions, from an age when faith and music were not yet separate things. For a maker who has long held sacred music close to his heart, rebuilding these instruments is also a way of answering that sound.

These instruments are still unfamiliar in Korea. All the more reason, then, to build them properly and to introduce them well.

Instruments

Three early instruments

Working from surviving technical drawings and historical sources, I design the workshop's own models — and give each instrument more than eight months of devoted work.

Violoncello da spalla

The cello of the shoulder

A small five-string cello slung from the shoulder, from the years when Bach was writing his cello suites. Held against the chest, its low register travels straight through the player's body. Cello range under violin fingering — an instrument still rarely seen in Korea.

Viola d'amore

The viola of love

Beneath the bowed strings run sympathetic strings that sound of their own accord. That resonance has been described as a small cathedral held inside the instrument. The blindfolded Amor carving and the flame soundholes make it as much a sculpture as an instrument.

The viol family — viola da gamba

Viols · Viola da gamba

Frets, a flat back and thin ribs — the sound that comes closest to the human voice. Intimate and warm rather than piercing. Viols come as a family, from treble to bass, so I build them as consort sets as well.

The Maker

Kyungsoo Jun

I have been making string instruments for sixteen years. Since 2018 I have taken part in China's international instrument exhibition every year, and many of the instruments I have built have gone to early-music players there — they are in use with ensembles in Chengdu, Shanghai and Guangzhou.

I do not thin the plates to make a new instrument sound impressive on its first day. I keep to the measurements the originals taught me through restoration work, and trust the instrument that deepens with time.

Kyungsoo Jun Luthier · Atelier VIOLZ, Suwon
About the maker

The Workshop

What the workshop keeps to

01

Wood

Only European tonewood dried for ten years or more. I do not build with lower grades — if the right piece is not there, I wait.

02

The curve of the plates

Graduation is what I spend the longest looking at. Dozens of measured points, checked against tap tone, in a workshop kept at a steady humidity.

03

Time

Six months for a violin, eight for a spalla or a d'amore. One instrument at a time.

04

Varnish

Varnished the traditional way, oil and spirit combined. I make Davide Sora's linoxin myself, and use only batches that have aged more than ten years.

05

The look of age

I also do antiquing, giving a new instrument the face of an old one. Layers of period pigment, wear where hands have rested, dust settled into the cracks of the varnish — time drawn onto a new instrument.

On Repair

Instruments that leave this workshop
stay in my care

As a rule I take on repair and maintenance only for instruments made here, so that commissioned work keeps its time. In return, every instrument that leaves the bench is looked after for good — from seasonal set-up adjustments to the restoration of damage the years leave behind.
